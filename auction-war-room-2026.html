Auction 101

Never done this before? Read this first

The one-sentence version: there are no draft slots — every player is available to every team, and the only thing standing between you and Ja'Marr Chase is how much of your $200 you're willing to spend.

How an auction works

Managers take turns nominating players. A nomination opens the bidding at $1 (or whatever the nominator opens with). Nomination order snakes around the room like a draft — but nominating a player does not mean you want him.
Everyone bids, highest bid wins. A short bid clock resets with each new bid. When it expires, the high bidder pays that price out of their $200 and the player is theirs.
This repeats until every roster is full. All 16 of your spots — starters, bench, even kicker and D/ST — are bought this way. Most of your bench will cost $1–3.
The math that governs everything: you must always be able to put at least $1 on each unfilled spot. So your max bid = money left − (open roster spots − 1). ESPN enforces this for you, but the good players track it for everyone else too.

The seven rules of your first auction

Write max prices before you draft. For every player you actually want, set the most you'll pay. In the room, never go more than $2–3 over — the moment you "just win the bid," you've lost the auction.
Nominate players you don't want. Early nominations should be big names you're avoiding — someone else's $46 on McCaffrey is your discount later.
Never show your hand early. Don't nominate your quiet targets while everyone still has fresh money. Let them surface after budgets have bled.
Cap the stars. Spending more than ~65–70% of your budget on three players is the classic first-auction blowup. Stars win leagues; three stars and thirteen $1 lottery tickets usually don't.
Watch the room's money, not just yours. ESPN shows every team's remaining budget. When the big spenders are tapped out, prices collapse — that's your buying window.
The mid-auction lull is where leagues are won. After the stars go, the room gets cautious and $25 players go for $17. Be the one still bidding.
Spend all of it. Unspent budget is points you left on the table. Ending the draft with $15 in hand is a loss, not discipline.

Tools

Nomination playbook & live budget math

What to nominate, in order

Openers (your first 3–4 turns): big names you're avoiding — McCaffrey, Achane, Saquon, Allen. Stars get paid no matter who calls the name; make sure it's not your money.
Early-middle: hyped names just below the elite — the room's favorite sleepers, rookies with buzz (Jeremiyah Love will start a war). Keep draining other budgets.
Middle: now float your real targets, one at a time, while the room licks its wounds. This is when Chase Brown goes for $24 and McConkey for $19.
Late: nominate kickers and defenses when other managers still have money to burn — every $2 they spend on a kicker is $2 that can't outbid your endgame sleepers.
One warning: "price enforcement" — bidding a player up just to cost a rival money — is how first-timers end up owning a $38 player they never wanted. Only bid what you'd happily pay.

Logistics

ESPN auction room notes & first-timer checklist

Know your ESPN room

  • Defaults: $200 budget, $1 minimum bid, all 16 roster spots filled by auction, nomination order snakes. Confirm the scoring is full PPR before anything else — dollar values shift with scoring just like rankings do.
  • ESPN enforces your max bid automatically (budget minus $1 per remaining spot), and displays every team's remaining budget — use that panel constantly; it's the whole game.
  • The bid clock is short and resets on every bid. Decide your ceiling before bidding starts, not during the countdown — the clock is designed to make you emotional.
  • Do one ESPN mock auction before the real one. The interface (nominate, quick-bid buttons, budget panel) is half the learning curve, and mocks are free reps.
  • If you disconnect, ESPN's auto-pilot bids conservatively off its own values — another reason to pre-rank your player list and keep the app installed as backup.
  • Roster math: 9 starters + 7 bench. About $180–190 of your $200 belongs on the 9 starters; bench spots should almost all be $1–3.

The last 24 hours

  • Print or split-screen the price board above and mark 20–25 targets across price bands with your personal max on each.
  • Re-check values the morning of the draft — August news moves auction prices just like ADP, and this page is a mid-August snapshot.
  • Scrub the injury report — Kittle (Achilles), LaPorta (back), Taylor's QB situation — before trusting any price here.
  • Plan two anchor paths: the room decides whether you get Gibbs at $60 or pivot to Taylor + McConkey for the same money. Both must be written down in advance.
  • Budget $8–12 for the endgame — that's what wins Diggs at $3 and Okonkwo at $2 while rivals are max-bid $1.
  • $1 on kicker, $1 on D/ST. No exceptions, no matter how the room behaves.
  • Expect to lose your favorite player. The plan isn't a roster, it's a set of prices. If the room pays over your caps all night, your team gets built in the middle rounds — and that team usually wins.
