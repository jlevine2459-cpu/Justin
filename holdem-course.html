Module 1

Thinking in EV

The mental rebuild. Before any specific play, you need the frame that every winning player shares: decisions are bets with prices, hands are ranges, and position is the biggest lever on the table.

Every decision is a price

Poker results are noisy; poker decisions are not. A decision is good if it makes money on average across all the ways the hand could play out — that average is its expected value (EV). You can lose a pot with a great decision and win one with a terrible decision, and over a session that noise dominates. Over ten thousand hands, only EV survives.

Concretely: the pot is $100 and your opponent is all-in for their last $50. You'll win 30% of the time. Folding has an EV of $0. Calling: 30% of the time you win $150, 70% of the time you lose $50.

EV(call) = 0.30 × $150 − 0.70 × $50 = $45 − $35 = +$10

Calling is correct even though you lose it most of the time. That sentence — correct even though you usually lose — is the single hardest thing for experienced-but-unstructured players to internalize, because memory keeps score in pots, not in EV.

Key idea

Judge yourself on decision quality, never on the outcome of one hand. If the price was right, the call was right — even when the river hurts.

You play ranges, not hands

Your opponent doesn't have "a hand." They have a range: the full set of hands they'd play this exact way, weighted by likelihood. When a tight player raises from first position, their range might be roughly 99+, A♣Q♣-type suited broadways, A K — and every read you make should narrow that set, never guess a single holding.

This cuts both ways. Observant opponents assign you a range too. Every action you take — the positions you raise from, the sizes you use, the hands you show down — teaches the table what your actions mean. Strategy is largely the discipline of making your range strong in the spots where you act aggressively.

From now on, replace the question "what does he have?" with two better ones:

  • "What is his range for playing this way?" — built from position, action, and player type.
  • "How does my hand do against that range?" — not against the scariest hand in it, and not against the weakest.

Position is the biggest lever

Acting last is a permanent structural edge. In position (IP), you see your opponent's decision before making yours every single street — you get information for free, you can bluff when they show weakness, value bet when they show strength is absent, and control the size of the pot. Out of position (OOP), you're guessing first all night.

This is why the button is the most profitable seat in poker and the blinds are the least — for everyone, at every skill level. The practical consequences:

  • Play many more hands from late position than early position (Module 2 makes this exact).
  • A marginal hand that's a fine open on the button is an easy fold under the gun.
  • Big pots OOP require bigger hands. Respect how much harder every street will be.

The two reasons to bet

Unstructured players bet for reasons like "to see where I'm at" or "to protect my hand" without finishing the thought. There are exactly two profitable reasons to put chips in:

  • Value: a worse hand will call. Ask literally: which worse hands call this bet? If you can't name them, it's not a value bet.
  • Bluff: a better hand will fold. Ask literally: which better hands fold? If none do, it's not a bluff — it's a donation.

"Protection" is real but it's a flavor of value betting (charging live draws), not a third reason. And "information" is the worst reason of all: the information costs a bet, arrives garbled, and a raise tells you mostly that you're now in a big pot with a medium hand.

Aggression, chosen — not constant

Winning players and losing players see roughly the same cards. The biggest statistical difference between them: winners raise or fold in spots where losers call. Calling is the passive middle — it can only win by having the best hand, while betting and raising add a second way to win: everyone folds. That extra way to win is where most of a good player's edge lives.

This does not mean bluffing wildly. It means that when you choose to play a hand, you usually play it as the aggressor, and when you can't stand a raise, you often shouldn't be calling either. "Tight and aggressive" is the durable formula: few hands, played hard.

Module 1 takeaway

EV over outcomes. Ranges over hands. Position over cards. Two reasons to bet. Raise-or-fold over call.

Module 2

Preflop Discipline

Preflop is the only street that happens every hand, and every later mistake compounds a preflop one. The fix is unglamorous: memorize sound opening ranges by position and follow them.

Why ranges beat feel

"I play it by feel" preflop really means: too many hands early, not enough hands on the button, and calls with pretty-looking dominated hands like K♣T♦ and A♠8♦ that win small pots and lose big ones. A fixed range chart eliminates the entire category of preflop error at the cost of a weekend of memorization. No other study hour in poker pays this well.

Sizing and the limping rule

  • Open size: 2.2–2.5bb online, ~3bb live (live games play looser, so charge more). Add 1bb per limper already in.
  • Never open-limp. Limping caps your winnings, invites multiway pots where your good hands shrink, and announces weakness. Every hand worth playing unopened is worth raising; everything else is a fold. One exception exists: completing the small blind in a limped multiway pot with a speculative hand.
  • Punish limpers. When players limp in front of you, raise your strong hands bigger (3bb + 1 per limper) — limpers' ranges are weak and capped, and you'll often take it down or play a bloated pot in position against a bad range.

Facing a raise: 3-bet or fold (mostly)

When someone opens in front of you, flat-calling should be your least common response outside the big blind. Cold-calling lets the blinds in cheap, caps your range, and surrenders initiative. Instead:

  • 3-bet your strong hands — QQ+, A K always, plus TT–JJ, A Q against late-position opens. Size it to about 3× the open in position, 4× out of position.
  • Add 3-bet bluffs from hands just below your calling range: suited aces like A♥5♥ and suited broadways. They block strong hands (holding an ace makes AA/AK less likely for them) and flop well when called.
  • Flat-call selectively in position with hands that play well multiway and can't stand a 3-bet being 4-bet: middling pairs (set-mining needs ~10:1 on implied odds — both stacks at least 10× the call), suited connectors, strong suited broadways.

Defending the big blind

The big blind is different: you already have 1bb invested and you close the action, so you're getting a discount everyone else isn't. Against a 2.5bb button open you risk 1.5bb to win 4bb — you need only ~27% equity to continue. That justifies defending wide: suited hands, connected hands, any pair, most aces. What it does not justify is defending trash like J♦3♣ or playing fit-or-fold after the flop. Defend wide, then play the later streets honestly.

From the small blind, do the opposite: you'll be out of position against everyone all hand, so play tight and aggressive — mostly raise or fold, almost never complete.

Module 2 takeaway

Memorize the RFI charts and obey them. Never open-limp. Versus opens: 3-bet or fold outside the blinds. Defend the big blind wide, the small blind tight.

Module 3

The Math That Pays

Four numbers cover 90% of in-game poker math: your outs, your equity, the pot odds you're offered, and the implied odds behind them. All of it is mental arithmetic you can do in under ten seconds.

Outs, and the rule of 2 and 4

An out is a card that turns your hand into (what you believe will be) the winner. Count them precisely, not optimistically:

DrawOutsExample
Flush draw9A♥8♥ on K♥6♥2♠
Open-ended straight draw89♠8♠ on 7♦6♣2♥
Gutshot4J♣T♣ on 8♦7♥2♠
Two overcards6A♣K♦ on 9♠6♥2♦
Flush draw + straight draw159♥8♥ on 7♥6♥K♠
Pair drawing to trips/two pair5A♦7♦ on 7♣K♠2♥ (two 7s, three aces)

Convert outs to winning chances with the rule of 2 and 4: with two cards to come (all-in on the flop), equity ≈ outs × 4. With one card to come, equity ≈ outs × 2. A flush draw on the flop facing a shove: 9 × 4 ≈ 36% (true value 35%). On the turn: 9 × 2 ≈ 18%. For big draws (9+ outs, two cards), subtract (outs − 8) for accuracy: 15 outs → 60 − 7 ≈ 53%.

Discount dirty outs. If the board is K♥Q♥4♠ and you hold J♦T♦, the 9♥ and A♥ "complete your straight" while completing someone's flush — count 6 clean outs, not 8.

Pot odds: the price of a call

Pot odds tell you the equity you need for a call to break even:

Required equity = call ÷ (pot after you call) = B ÷ (P + 2B)

where P is the pot before the bet and B is the bet you're facing. The common cases are worth memorizing cold:

Bet sizeYou needRough translation
⅓ pot20%Call with almost any real piece or draw
½ pot25%Call with pairs, good draws, good overcards
⅔ pot28.5%Flush draws yes; naked gutshots need help
Pot33%Only strong hands and strong draws continue
2× pot40%You need to beat a real hand often — or fold

Note what this table quietly proves: a call doesn't need to win most of the time to be right. Facing a half-pot bet, winning 1 time in 3 prints money.

Implied odds — and their evil twin

Implied odds are the extra money you expect to win on later streets when you hit. They justify calls that raw pot odds don't — a gutshot getting the wrong immediate price can still be profitable if hitting it wins a stack. Implied odds are best when your draw is hidden (straights beat flushes here — the third heart alarms everyone; the straight card doesn't), when stacks are deep, and when the opponent has a strong hand they'll pay you with.

Reverse implied odds is the money you lose when you hit and are still second-best. Weak flushes, bottom-end straights, and dominated top pairs (K♠T♣ hitting a king against A♦K♦) are the classic carriers. Hands that make second-best hands are the most expensive hands in poker — this is exactly why the Module 2 ranges exclude them.

Module 3 takeaway

Outs × 4 (two cards) or × 2 (one card) ≈ equity. Required equity = bet ÷ (pot + 2 × bet). Hidden draws and deep stacks raise implied odds; dominated hands carry reverse implied odds.

Module 4

The Flop

Three cards land and two ranges collide. Reading which range a flop favors — and betting accordingly — is the core postflop skill, and it's more about the board than about your hand.

Board texture: the first read

Before thinking about your own hand, classify the flop:

  • Dry / static: K♠7♦2♣, A♣8♠3♥. No flushes or straights possible, few draws. Whoever is ahead now tends to stay ahead; turn and river rarely change anything.
  • Wet / dynamic: 9♥8♥6♠, J♠T♠7♦. Draws everywhere; the best hand now is fragile and equities run close together. Big cards will change the winner constantly.

Static boards reward small bets and patience; dynamic boards reward big bets — charging draws now — and reward having the draws yourself.

Range advantage: whose flop is it?

Ask: whose preflop range connects better with this board? If you raised from early position and the flop comes A♦K♠4♣, that board smashes your range (you have all the AK, AA, KK, AQ) and misses the big blind's (they 3-bet those hands or don't have them). You can bet almost your entire range small and print. Flip it: on 7♠6♠5♥, the big blind's defend range (suited connectors, middling cards) just got well acquainted with the board, and your A♣Q♦ should tread carefully.

Two related concepts:

  • Range advantage — whose whole range has more equity. Drives how often you bet.
  • Nut advantage — who holds more of the very strongest hands. Drives how big you can bet.

The continuation bet, rebuilt

You raised preflop, one caller, flop arrives. The c-bet decision tree:

SituationPlayWhy
Dry board that favors your range (K♦7♣2♠ as preflop raiser)Bet small — 25–33% pot — with nearly everythingTheir range missed; a small bet folds out their air and taxes their weak pairs cheaply. Your worst hands profit most.
Wet board, you have the strong hands and big drawsBet big — 66–75% pot — with strong hands and good draws; check the middleCharge their draws, deny equity, build the pot while ahead. Polarize: value + draws bet, medium showdown hands check.
Board that favors the caller (6♥5♥4♣ vs a big blind defend)Check a lot, even strong overpairs sometimesNo range advantage means no automatic c-bet. Betting "because you raised preflop" is a habit, not a reason.
Multiway pot (2+ callers)Bet strictly for value; bluff rarelySomeone usually has a piece. C-bet bluffs that work heads-up burn money multiway.

Facing the c-bet

The same logic in reverse. When the preflop raiser makes a small c-bet on a dry board, they're doing it with everything — so their range is wide and weak, and your response shouldn't be fit-or-fold:

  • Raise with your strong hands and your best draws (pair + draw, big combo draws). This is where check-raising earns its keep: it punishes automatic c-bettors and makes your draws two-way weapons.
  • Call ("float") in position with backdoor draws, overcards, and pairs — planning to take the pot away when they give up on the turn, which auto-c-bettors do constantly.
  • Fold the true nothing. No shame; the ⅓-pot price means you only need 20%, but zero is still less than 20.
Module 4 takeaway

Classify the board before your hand. Bet small and often on boards that favor your range; big and selectively on wet boards; rarely into ranges that beat yours or into multiple players. Check-raise the auto-c-bettors.

Module 5

Turn & River

The pots get real. Late streets are where value betting pays your rent, where bluffs need a résumé, and where hand reading finally cashes in the range you tracked from preflop.

Hand reading: narrow, never widen

The professional habit that most separates strong players: track the opponent's range street by street and only remove hands. Preflop action sets the range; the flop call trims it (drop the air they'd fold); the turn raise trims again (keep only hands that raise here). By the river, "what can he have?" often has a shockingly short answer — and one rule enforces it all: a range never grows. If the story requires a hand they'd have played differently earlier, the story is a bluff.

Practical anchor: when a passive player raises the turn or river, they are almost never bluffing. The bottom of their raising range is usually two pair. Fold your one-pair hands and feel nothing.

Barreling the turn

You c-bet the flop, got called, turn arrives. Continue betting ("barrel") when at least one is true:

  • Your equity is real: you have a strong hand, or a draw with 8+ outs that can improve to the best hand if called.
  • The card helps your range more than theirs: an ace or king on the turn after you raised preflop is a fine barrel card even when it misses you — it hits your story. A card completing their draws is not.
  • Their flop-calling range was full of one-and-done hands: weak pairs and draws that fold to real turn pressure.

The anti-pattern: bet flop, give up turn, every single time you miss. Observant opponents float you once and take the pot away forever. If you weren't planning any turns to barrel, the flop c-bet was probably wrong too.

Thin value: the amateur's missing salary

The single most profitable habit you can add from this course: bet good-but-not-great hands on the river for value. With A♠J♠ on J♦8♣3♥2♦9♣, checking "to be safe" against an opponent who'd call with J♥T♥, Q♣J♣, or a stubborn 9♠8♠ burns money every time. The test is Module 1's question, asked honestly: which worse hands call? If the answer is "several," bet — half to two-thirds pot — and get paid. Being check-raised occasionally is the small tax on a large income.

"Pot control" is for medium hands facing uncertainty, not a lifestyle. Players who never get thin value can only profit from their monsters, and monsters are rare.

Bluffing with a résumé

A river bluff is a claim: "I hold the strong hand this line represents." Good bluffs come with credentials:

  • A consistent story. Your bets on every street must make sense as the value hand you're claiming. A river bomb after checking flop and turn claims a hand that plays that way — usually nothing does.
  • Blockers. Bluff with cards that make their calling hands less likely. Holding A♥ on a three-heart board blocks the nut flush — the best bluffing hand is one that prevents them from having the hand they'd call with.
  • The right victim. Bluff players who can fold, in spots where their range is capped. Module 6 covers who those are — and who they aren't.
  • Failed draws as raw material. Your missed straight and flush draws arrived at the river with no showdown value and a story already told. They're your natural bluffs; random air is not.

The arithmetic of bluffing is friendlier than it feels: a pot-sized bluff needs to work just 50% of the time to break even; a half-pot bluff only 33%.

Sizing tells a story

  • Big bets (75%–150% pot) are polar: the very strong and the bluffs. Use them when your range holds the nuts and theirs is capped.
  • Small bets (25–40% pot) are merged: lots of medium-strong value that wants a cheap call. Use them to tax weaker showdown hands.
  • Pick a size for your whole range in a spot, not per hand — sizing your monsters big and your medium hands small is a tell that good opponents read like a subtitle.
Module 5 takeaway

Ranges only narrow. Barrel with equity or a story, not on autopilot. Bet thin for value relentlessly. Bluff with blockers, consistent lines, and failed draws — against people who fold.

Module 6

Reading the Room

Baseline strategy keeps you solvent; exploitation makes you rich. Most opponents have loud, stable leaks — your job is to notice them fast and swing hard at exactly those leaks.

The four players you'll actually meet

TypeSignatureYour exploit
The Nit (tight-passive)Folds for an hour, then suddenly raisesSteal their blinds relentlessly. When they wake up with aggression, believe them — fold hands as strong as top pair. Never pay off their river raises.
The Calling Station (loose-passive)Calls everything, folds nothing, loves any pair or drawNever bluff. Ever. Value bet three streets thinner than feels polite — top pair weak kicker is a monster here. Their calls are the game's steadiest income.
The TAG (tight-aggressive)Solid ranges, positional awareness — plays like this courseSmallest edges here; mostly stay out of the way. Attack their known folding spots (their blinds, their capped checking ranges) and avoid paying off their value.
The Maniac (loose-aggressive)Raises constantly, barrels everythingTighten up, then call down with good one-pair hands and let them bluff into you. Trap instead of raising them off their bluffs. Variance goes up; so does profit.

Classify everyone within two orbits using two questions: how many hands do they play? (loose/tight) and do they bet or call? (aggressive/passive). Then re-classify when evidence changes — players tilt, and a nit three buy-ins down stops being a nit.

Low-stakes population reads

Until proven otherwise, assume the room plays like this — because on average, it does:

  • Big river bets and raises are value. Low-stakes players under-bluff the river massively. Your beautiful hero-calls are lighting money on fire; your disciplined folds are printing it.
  • They call flops too wide and fold turns too much. Second barrels on good cards are wildly profitable against this pattern.
  • Limping means weakness, and limp-raising means aces. The strongest sentence in live poker.
  • They don't fold pairs. Adjust bluffing down and value betting up across the board. When in doubt at low stakes: less bluffing, more value.
  • Sizing is honest. Most players bet big with big hands and small with weak ones. Until someone shows you a small-bet monster or an overbet bluff, take their sizes at face value.

Live tells worth trusting

Physical tells are the least reliable read — behind position, action, and sizing — but a few carry real signal, mostly variations on one principle: relaxation means strength, tension means weakness. A talkative player who goes quiet, a bettor whose hands tremble (adrenaline from a big hand, not fear), someone who snap-calls (a draw — no decision needed), someone staring you down after betting (usually weak; the comfortable ones look away). Weight them lightly, and only when they contradict nothing else.

Meanwhile, mind your own: fixed bet-sizing habits, instant actions with obvious decisions, and reaching for chips before your turn all leak information. Decide everything on a steady internal count.

Module 6 takeaway

Two questions classify anyone. Value-bet stations, believe nits, dodge TAGs, call down maniacs. At low stakes: bluff less, value bet thinner, and believe the big river raise.

Module 7

The Long Game

Strategy loses to bankroll ruin and tilt more often than it loses to better players. This module is the guardrails: money management, emotional management, and a study loop that compounds.

Bankroll: survive the noise

Variance in no-limit is brutal and non-negotiable: a solid winning player can run break-even for tens of thousands of hands. The bankroll rules exist so that this noise can never knock you out of the game:

  • Cash games: 20–40 buy-ins for your regular stake (recreational with income: 20 is fine; this is your income: 40+).
  • Tournaments: 100+ buy-ins. MTT variance dwarfs cash variance — even great players go 30 tournaments without a real cash.
  • Move down without ego. Dropping stakes after losses isn't failure; it's the system working. The players who refuse to move down are the ones who go broke.
  • Poker money is not spending money. Separate it fully. A bankroll you can't rebuild deserves stakes it can't feel.

Tilt: name it to tame it

Tilt is any emotional state that changes your decisions — and it wears more costumes than anger. The common ones: injustice tilt ("I can't win a flip"), entitlement tilt ("I deserve this pot, I played it perfectly"), revenge tilt (targeting the player who sucked out), and winner's tilt (playing loose and fancy because you're up). The protocol:

  1. Pre-commit to a stop-loss — e.g., three buy-ins or a two-bad-beats-in-ten-minutes rule. Decide it before the session, when you're sane.
  2. Catch the physical signs — heat, rushing decisions, narrating bad luck to yourself — and take a walk at the first one, not the fourth.
  3. Reframe with Module 1: the bad beat means you got the money in good. That's the job. The variance is where your profit comes from — bad players only keep playing because they sometimes win.

Game selection: the legal cheat code

Your winrate is set less by how well you play than by who you play against. The ninth-best player in the world loses money at a table with the top eight. Choose tables with visible limpers, big average pots, and drinks on the rail; change seats to get position on the money (loose players on your right, so you act after them); and leave good games reluctantly, bad games instantly. Table selection feels like it isn't poker. It's the most profitable skill in poker.

A study loop that compounds

  1. During play, mark hands — any pot where you felt unsure, win or lose. One line of notes is enough.
  2. After the session, review two or three of them cold: rebuild the ranges street by street, redo the math from Module 3, decide what the best line was.
  3. Keep a leak list. Patterns will emerge ("I pay off river raises," "I open too loose in the hijack"). Pick one leak per week and make it your only focus at the table.
  4. Ratio: roughly 4 hours playing to 1 hour studying. Playing volume without review repeats mistakes at scale; study without volume never gets tested.
Module 7 takeaway

20–40 buy-ins for cash, 100+ for tournaments, move down without ego. Pre-commit a stop-loss and respect it. Pick tables like your winrate depends on it — it does. Review a few marked hands after every session.
